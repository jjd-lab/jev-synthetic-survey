An evaluation · Twin-2K-500 · jev-1.13.0 vs gpt-4.1

Does a native probability beat a verbalized one?

We asked TypeSafe’s Jev, a decision-only model, and gpt-4.1 to play the same 300 survey respondents. Jev returns a typed value and a probability per option and generates no text at all, so its vector is native rather than written. We asked the chat model to state one in words. Two tests were written down first. Both failed, so the strong claim does not hold. It failed on one of the six measures reported; Jev won the other five, at a thirty-fourth of the cost, and that soft claim is the interesting one.

The registered arm is Jev Choice against verbalized gpt-4.1 probabilities, and the measure it loses is the two-option distribution gap the test turns on. Re-asked as a Noul it wins that measure too, but after the fact, so the failure stands. The headline arms were then re-run over all 2,058 respondents and the numbers held: 0.1530 to 0.1523 on the distribution gap, 0.1472 to 0.1418 on calibration, 67.28% to 67.64% on accuracy, across a 6.9-fold increase in sample. What follows teaches the instrument, then the full-sample gpt-4.1 run, then that comparison.

01

What a synthetic survey respondent is

A language model is given a few facts about a real person, walked through questions that person also answered, and scored against those answers. Real panels are slow. A model that could stand in for one would let you pretest a survey. Whether it can is the measurement.

One cell · pricing block · QID9-style

A held-out yes/no, with a piped price

Please consider the following product category: butter. You see Land O Lakes Salted Stick Butter, 16 oz, 4 sticks, priced at $6.49. Would you or would you not purchase this product?
  • Yes, I would purchase the product
  • No, I would not purchase the product

Forty of the 108 scored columns are this task. The price is randomized per person, which is why every figure in the repo is averaged by task first.

02

gpt-4.1 on all 2,058 people

Before the Jev comparison, the same engine walked gpt-4.1 through the whole Twin-2K-500 sample, three times. This chapter has no Jev in it. It asks what the persona is worth.

Landing near a published figure while losing to the column’s own modal answer is the whole reading. A demographics-grounded twin is usable for a population’s distribution, not for predicting a specific person.

03

Jev against gpt-4.1

Same 300 respondents, same 108 columns, same stateful demographics persona. The test that decides it is a conjunction: Jev must win both the 65 yes/no columns and the 43 ordinal ones. It loses the yes/no half. The second test takes TypeSafe at its word: System One models are “trained for calibrated decisions”, so the bar was set at 0.05. No arm comes close — Jev Noul 0.1472, Jev Choice 0.2029, gpt-4.1 0.2393 — and Jev is the better calibrated of the two models, by 39%, while still sitting three times over the bar. TypeSafe qualifies the claim itself, and the qualification is the finding: “calibration is measured across groups of predictions; it does not guarantee that an individual answer is correct.” Failing for every arm at once is what makes this a statement about demographics-only grounding rather than about Jev.

Distribution gap, yes/no: Jev Choice 0.1985 against gpt-4.1 0.1789. Wilcoxon p = 1.0000. Jev worse. The ordinal half goes the other way, 0.6864 against 0.7272, p = 0.031.

Accuracy is last on purpose. The arm that wins it, gpt-4.1 hard answer at 69.32%, is last on the distribution gap. All five land under 73.59% on this 300-person slice, the score from ignoring the persona entirely.

04

Where Jev is still the interesting option

The failure of the strong claim stands. These are the measured reasons it is not the end of the story. The last paragraph on this page is opinion, and is labeled as such.

Author’s take · not a result

I think Jev is a strong option and I would use it. It costs about 34 times less, it produced no errors in 24,596 cells, it wins the ordinal half outright, and the yes/no half it lost closes almost entirely once you stop asking those questions the wrong way. I would use it today for ordinal-scale marginals under cost pressure, with Noul for anything yes/no.

05

What this does not say

  • The 300 are the first 300 rows of the sample — older, whiter, more conservative. Read the paired comparisons. Do not read any absolute number as a population estimate.
  • The holdout is a cognitive-bias battery. Answers are not supposed to track who you are. Individual prediction fails for every arm.
  • Noul was built after seeing the result. Option descriptions were pre-registered later and changed nothing.
  • Jev was given the same plain input gpt-4.1 got. TypeSafe’s own prompting levers are mostly untested, not refuted.
  • Neither arm should be used for demographic crosstabs. The results above are at the population and per-person levels, which is where the criteria were set. Between demographic groups both arms fail, in opposite directions: Jev reproduces about 40% of the real gap, gpt-4.1 about 2.7× too much on politics. That is a reason not to use either for breakdowns, not a result about the models.
